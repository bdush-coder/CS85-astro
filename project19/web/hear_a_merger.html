STEP 2 OF 6

Meet the two black holes

A black hole is what’s left when a giant star collapses: so much mass squeezed so small that nothing, not even light, can escape its pull. The two in this collision were each roughly 30 times the Sun’s mass, but only about 200 km across.

STEP 3 OF 6

How it becomes a sound

Moving masses make gravitational waves: ripples in space itself. As one passes, it stretches space one way and squeezes it the other way, then switches, once per vibration. In the panel, the curved arms spiralling out are those ripples. Two crests leave for every orbit.

LIGO has two 4 km tubes at right angles, with lasers measuring their lengths. When the ripple from these black holes passed Earth, one tube got slightly longer while the other got shorter, back and forth. The change was about 4 × 10⁻¹⁸ m, roughly 1/400 of the width of a proton. The small L in the corner of the panel shows it, made about 10²⁰ times bigger.

LIGO writes down that stretching 4,096 times every second. Those numbers rise and fall 35 to 250 times a second, which is the same rate as sounds we can hear. Send them to a speaker and the speaker moves the same way: you hear the chirp.

Is it really sound? No. Sound can’t travel through empty space. These are ripples in space itself, and their frequencies happen to fall in the range human ears can hear. So we can feed the detector data straight into a speaker and hear it, which is what your WAV file does.
STEP 4 OF 6

Why the pitch rises

The picture under the black holes is real LIGO data, the same view as your first plot (plots/19_gw150914_qtransform.png). Time runs left to right and pitch runs bottom to top. Brighter color means a stronger signal. The bright curved streak is the chirp. The white dashed line is the formula’s prediction, and the dot is “now”. Tap a sentence to jump there:

  1. Two black holes, each roughly 30 times the Sun’s mass, were orbiting each other.
  2. The orbit sends out gravitational waves, which carry energy away.
  3. Losing energy makes the black holes spiral closer together, and closer orbits are faster.
  4. Gravitational waves vibrate twice per orbit, so a faster orbit means a higher pitch and a louder signal. That’s the upward sweep.
  5. When the two black holes merge into one, the signal dies away, which is why the streak ends abruptly just after 0.

From the explanation you got when you first ran project 19.

In about 0.1 seconds the signal sweeps from about 35 Hz, a low hum, up to about 250–300 Hz, and then it stops. Watch the readouts. In the last tenth of a second the black holes go from about 600 km apart to touching, and their speed climbs past a third of the speed of light.

STEP 5 OF 6

How the sound disappears

They merge. When the two black holes touch, they become one black hole of 61.5 Suns. It starts out lopsided and wobbly, and for a moment it “rings” like a struck bell, at about 255 Hz.

The ringing dies within milliseconds. A black hole quickly settles into a smooth, round shape, and a smooth round object spinning steadily sends out no waves. Each 4 ms the ringing shrinks to about a third of its size, so after about 0.02 s it’s gone. No waves means nothing for LIGO to record: the chirp stops, and only the hiss is left.

Where did the energy go? The two black holes added up to 64.5 Suns, but the new one is only 61.5. About 3.0 Suns’ worth of mass was turned into gravitational-wave energy and carried away by the ripples.

Use 250× slower to see the last moments: the two become one, wobble, and go still. The spiral waves keep moving outward afterwards. That’s the last ripple leaving, on its 1.3-billion-year trip to Earth.

The answer to question 19

In LIGO’s Livingston detector, the first black-hole collision sounded like a short, rising “whoop”. Over about 0.2 seconds it slid up from a low hum near 35 Hz to about 250 Hz, getting louder as it went, and then stopped dead. All of it was buried in a steady hiss of detector noise.

The rise in pitch is the two black holes spiralling closer and orbiting faster. The sudden stop is the merger: they became one black hole that rang for a few milliseconds and then went quiet.

Words to know

Glossary

Black hole
What’s left when a giant star collapses: so much mass in so small a space that nothing, not even light, escapes.
Gravitational wave
A ripple in space itself, made by moving masses. It stretches and squeezes everything it passes through, by a tiny amount.
LIGO
Two detectors in the US (Livingston, Louisiana and Hanford, Washington). Each has two 4 km arms, with lasers measuring changes in their lengths.
Hz (hertz)
Vibrations per second. Human hearing runs from about 20 Hz (deep bass) to 20,000 Hz.
Chirp
A signal that rises in pitch, like a bird’s chirp. The rise is the black holes orbiting faster as they spiral in.
Merger and ringdown
The moment two black holes become one, then the brief “bell ringing” as the new black hole settles down.
Spectrogram
A picture of sound: time left to right, pitch bottom to top, brightness = how strong.
Signal-to-noise ratio
How strongly a signal stands out from the detector’s noise. Bigger is clearer.
